    Exercises 5.1: Types of Radioactivity

    1. Particle notation.

    Give the nuclear symbol, mass number, and charge of an alpha particle.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\text{;}\) mass number 4, charge \(2+\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Beta identity.

    What particle is emitted in beta decay?
    🔗
    Answer.
    An electron, conventionally written \(^{0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Gamma properties.

    Does a gamma photon carry electric charge or mass?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Neither; its charge and mass number are zero.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Positron.

    How does a positron differ from an electron in charge and mass?
    🔗
    Answer.
    It has the same mass but opposite (positive) charge.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Charge ranking.

    Order beta, gamma, and alpha radiation by electric charge.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Beta: \(1-\text{;}\) gamma: 0; alpha: \(2+\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Mass comparison.

    Which has greater particle mass, an alpha particle or a beta particle?
    🔗
    Answer.
    The alpha particle.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Lacks a charge.

    Which of alpha, beta, positrons, protons, neutrons and gamma radiation lacks an electrical charge?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Gamma radiation, and neutrons
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8. Atomic effect.

    Which listed emission leaves both atomic number and mass number unchanged?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Gamma emission.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9. Misconception.

    Does beta emission mean an orbital electron simply escaped from an otherwise unchanged nucleus?
    🔗
    Answer.
    No. A nuclear neutron converts to a proton, with an electron being emitted.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    10. Identification.

    A nuclear emission has mass number of 1 and charge \(1+\text{.}\) Identify it.
    🔗
    Answer.
    A proton
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises 5.2: Balancing Decay Reactions

    1. Alpha equation.

    Complete \(^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U}\rightarrow\,\underline{\hspace{1cm}}+{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(^{234}_{90}\mathrm{Th}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Beta daughter reasoning.

    Phosphorus-32 emits a beta particle. Predict the resulting isotope and explain why the atomic number rises although the mass number stays fixed.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The daughter is \(^{32}_{16}\mathrm{S}\text{.}\) A neutron converts to a proton which affects the atomic number. The mass of a proton an neutron are essentially the same, so the mass number doesn’t change.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Positron verification.

    A proposed positron decay is \(^{18}_{9}\mathrm{F}\rightarrow{}^{18}_{10}\mathrm{Ne}+{}^{0}_{1}\mathrm{e}\text{.}\) Correct it.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The resulting isotope must be \(^{18}_{8}\mathrm{O}\) because \(9=8+1\text{;}\) mass number remains 18.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Gamma equation.

    Write the gamma emission of excited technetium-99, denoted \(^{99m}_{43}\mathrm{Tc}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(^{99m}_{43}\mathrm{Tc}\rightarrow{}^{99}_{43}\mathrm{Tc}+\gamma\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Missing emission.

    Identify the emission in \(^{210}_{84}\mathrm{Po}\rightarrow{}^{206}_{82}\mathrm{Pb}+\underline{\hspace{1cm}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    An alpha particle, \(^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Conservation check.

    Why is \(^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\rightarrow{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}+{}^{0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}\) not a balanced beta-decay equation?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Atomic-number sums differ; the product isotope should have atomic number 7.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Sequential decay.

    A nucleus undergoes alpha decay then beta decay. What are the net changes in mass and atomic number?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Mass number decreases by 4; atomic number decreases by 1.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8. Electron capture.

    Electron capture is a process that can occur in the nucleus of certain atoms. It follows the same rules as radioactive decay, but in the opposite direction. Complete \(^{7}_{4}\mathrm{Be}+{}^{0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}\rightarrow\underline{\hspace{1cm}}\) in simplified notation.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(^{7}_{3}\mathrm{Li}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9. Reverse inference.

    \(^{222}_{86}\mathrm{Rn}\) is produced from an alpha decay reaction. Find the parent isotope.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    10. Element change.

    Why does gamma emission not turn one element into another?
    🔗
    Answer.
    It changes nuclear energy, not proton count.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises 5.3: Uses of Radioactive Materials

    1. Chain reaction.

    Explain how neutrons sustain a fission chain reaction.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Neutrons released by one fission event can trigger fission in additional nuclei.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Control rods.

    What role do neutron-absorbing control rods play in a reactor?
    🔗
    Answer.
    They reduce available neutrons and control the chain reaction rate.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Tracer.

    Why can a small radioactive tracer help locate a substance within a body?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Its emitted radiation can be detected outside the body to reveal its distribution.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. PET signal.

    What produces the photons detected in positron emission tomography?
    🔗
    Answer.
    A positron annihilates with an electron, typically producing two gamma photons.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Targeting.

    Why might a glucose-like tracer accumulate more strongly in some tumors?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Some tumors have elevated glucose uptake
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Therapy versus imaging.

    How does using radiation to destroy tumor cells differ from using a tracer to image them?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Therapy aims to deliver damaging energy; imaging aims to detect distribution but with less damaging quantities of radiation
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Isotope choice.

    Why is a very long-lived tracer often undesirable for short diagnostic imaging?
    🔗
    Answer.
    It can continue irradiating the person after useful imaging has ended.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8. Energy pathway.

    Trace the main energy conversions from fission to electricity in a typical power plant.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Nuclear energy becomes heat, then turbine mechanical energy, then electrical energy.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9. Sterilization.

    How can radiation sterilize equipment without heating it to a high temperature?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Ionizing radiation damages microorganisms and their molecular machinery.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises 5.4: Measuring radioactivity

    1. Activity conversion.

    Convert 2.00 Ci to Bq using \(1\,\mathrm{Ci}=3.70\times10^{10}\) Bq.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(7.40\times10^{10}\) Bq.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Reverse conversion.

    Convert \(7.40\times10^7\) Bq to mCi.
    🔗
    Answer.
    2.00 mCi.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Disintegration count.

    A source has constant activity 500. Bq over 10.0 s. How many decays are expected?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(5.00\times10^3\) decays.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Activity meaning.

    What is the definition of one Bq?
    🔗
    Answer.
    One nuclear decay per second.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Conversions.

    If a patient’s body aborbs 3.1mGy of proton radiation, how many Sv did they absorb? Is the person likely fine, soon to be sick, or soon to be dead?
    🔗
    Answer.
    0.031 Sv; They are close to increasing their cancer risk, but they are below the 0.5 Sv threshold for radiation sickness.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Absorbed dose.

    Convert 250. rad to Gy using 100 rad = 1 Gy.
    🔗
    Answer.
    2.50 Gy
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Equivalent dose.

    Convert 75.0 mrem to mSv using 100 rem = 1 Sv.
    🔗
    Answer.
    0.750 mSv.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8. Quantity distinction.

    Explain the difference between activity and absorbed dose.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Activity measures decay rate; absorbed dose measures energy deposited per mass.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9. Biological weighting.

    Why might equal absorbed doses of different radiation types have different biological effects?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Radiation types have different biological effectiveness, represented by weighting factors.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    10. Energy per mass.

    A person absorbs 15 rad of alpha particles. What is that equivalent to in Sv? Also, is the person likely fine, soon to be sick, or soon to be dead?
    🔗
    Answer.
    3Sv, they likely experience a fatal dose (a dose of 3-4 Sv is lethal to half of the people exposed to that amount).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    11. Comparison.

    Which source has greater activity: 1.00 microcurie or \(5.00\times10^4\) Bq?
    🔗
    Answer.
    The latter; 1.00 microcurie is \(3.70\times10^4\) Bq.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises 5.5: Radiation Safety

    1. Time.

    For a fixed dose rate, what happens to exposure when time is halved?
    🔗
    Answer.
    The dose is halved.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Distance.

    For an unshielded point source, what happens to intensity when distance doubles?
    🔗
    Answer.
    It falls to one quarter by the inverse-square relationship.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Shielding.

    Which is more easily stopped by paper, alpha or gamma radiation?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Alpha radiation.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Internal contamination.

    Why is preventing inhalation of radioactive dust important?
    🔗
    Answer.
    It prevents radioactive material from becoming an internal source of exposure.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Practical control.

    How can remote handling tools reduce exposure?
    🔗
    Answer.
    They increase distance from the source and may allow the presence of shielding between the operator and the material.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Inverse distance.

    A point-source dose rate is 8.0 mrad/h at 1.0 m. What is it at 4.0 m under the same assumptions?
    🔗
    Answer.
    0.50 mrad/h.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Dose calculation.

    At a hypothetical constant dose rate of 0.20 mSv/h, what dose accumulates in 15 min?
    🔗
    Answer.
    0.050 mSv.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8. Combined controls.

    Time is halved and distance from an unshielded point source is doubled. What fraction of the original dose remains?
    🔗
    Answer.
    One eighth.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9. Protective clothing.

    Does wearing rubber gloves provide substantial shielding from penetrating gamma radiation?
    🔗
    Answer.
    No. Gloves mainly help prevent contamination; penetrating radiation requires appropriate shielding.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises 5.6: Half-life of Radioisotopes

    1. Whole half-lives.

    An 80.0 mg sample undergoes three half-lives of decay. Find the amount remaining.
    🔗
    Answer.
    10.0 mg.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Elapsed time.

    A nuclide has half-life 6.00 h. How long is required for activity to fall to one quarter?
    🔗
    Answer.
    12.0 h.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Half-life inference.

    Activity drops from 160 Bq to 20 Bq in 9.0 days. Find the half-life.
    🔗
    Answer.
    3.0 days.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Fractional time.

    Find the fraction remaining after half a half-life.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(2^{-1/2}\approx0.707\text{,}\) or 70.7 percent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Reverse amount.

    A sample contains 5.00 mg after two half-lives. What was its starting amount?
    🔗
    Answer.
    20.0 mg.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Decayed fraction.

    What percentage of a radioactive sample remains after four half-lives?
    🔗
    Answer.
    93.75 percent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Different nuclides.

    Two equally active sources have half-lives of 1 h and 10 h. Which has higher activity after 5 h?
    🔗
    Answer.
    The 10 h source.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8. Activity model.

    A source starts at 25000 Bq with half-life 2.00 days. Find activity after 7.00 days.
    🔗
    Answer.
    2200 Bq.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9. Misconception.

    Does every remaining nucleus necessarily decay during the next half-life?
    🔗
    Answer.
    No. Decay is probabilistic; half the population is expected to remain.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    10. Threshold.

    How many whole half-lives are needed for less than 1 percent to remain?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Seven, leaving about 0.781 percent.
    🔗
